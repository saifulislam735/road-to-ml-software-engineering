§ 01 — Foundation

Planning & Product Thinking

Every successful SaaS starts with questions, not code. The engineers who skip this phase rewrite their entire system 6 months later. This is what senior engineers and CTOs think through before touching a keyboard.

Answer these before writing a single line of code. Experienced founders spend 2–4 weeks here.

Business Questions

Who is my customer? What exact pain do they have today? Why would they pay for my solution instead of building it themselves? What is my pricing model — per-seat, usage-based, flat fee, freemium? What's my go-to-market channel?

Technical Scale Questions

How many users in year 1? Year 3? What's the read/write ratio of my data? Is my workload CPU-bound, I/O-bound, or memory-bound? Do I need real-time features? What does a peak traffic day look like?

Multi-tenancy Questions

Am I selling to individuals, teams, or enterprises? Do tenants need data isolation guarantees? Do I need per-tenant customization? Do customers want their own domain? Do they need to control their own infrastructure?

Regulatory Questions

Do I handle payments? (PCI-DSS) Health data? (HIPAA) EU customers? (GDPR) Government customers? (FedRAMP, SOC2) These determine your entire security and data architecture from day one.

The #1 planning mistake: Building for 10 million users before you have 10 paying customers. You cannot predict your actual scaling bottlenecks until you have real traffic. Build for 10× your current scale, not 10,000×.
Week 1–2

Define the Core Loop

What is the single action that delivers value to a user? For Shopify: list a product → customer buys → merchant gets paid. That's the loop. Everything else is enhancement. Build ONLY the loop first.

Week 2–4

Feature Ruthlessness

For every feature ask: "Can we launch without this?" If yes, it's post-launch. Multi-currency? Post-launch. Analytics dashboard? Post-launch. One-click import? Post-launch. You need: auth + core CRUD + billing + deployment. Nothing else.

Week 4–8

Technical Debt Budget

Consciously decide what shortcuts you're taking: no test suite (acceptable for MVP, not for scale), no observability (acceptable pre-launch, not post-launch), hardcoded config (never acceptable, even at MVP stage). Write these down. They are debts with interest rates.

Launch Gate

The 5 Non-Negotiables at Launch

1) Auth that doesn't leak sessions. 2) Payments that don't lose money. 3) Data that doesn't corrupt. 4) HTTPS everywhere. 5) At least one alert that wakes you up when things break. Without these, you're not ready.

❌ Premature Microservices

Teams split their MVP into 12 microservices before having product-market fit. When requirements change (they always do), every change requires coordinating N services. Start with a monolith. Always.

❌ Wrong Tech Stack for the Team

The "best" technology is the one your team knows deeply. A senior Python team shipping a Django monolith will outrun a junior team learning Go microservices every time. Boring tech wins.

❌ No Observability at Launch

Launching without error tracking, structured logging, and at least one uptime alert means you learn about production incidents from angry customers. You need to know before they do.

❌ Multi-tenancy as an Afterthought

This is the most expensive retrofit in SaaS. If you know you're building multi-tenant, add store_id to every table on day one. Retrofitting this into an existing schema costs weeks of migration work.

Senior engineers don't just pick architectures — they understand the exact tradeoffs of each choice relative to their constraints. Here is the complete decision framework used by Staff Engineers at modern SaaS companies.

How real SaaS systems evolve — not how they're designed in textbooks.

Stage 0 — Launch (Day 1 to $10K MRR)

One VPS, one process, one database

A $6/mo Hetzner VPS or Railway instance. Postgres managed by Neon or Railway. Node/Python/Go app server. Nginx in front. Zero operational overhead. You should be able to redeploy in under 2 minutes. Shopify started exactly like this. Stripe started exactly like this.

Stage 1 — Growth ($10K–100K MRR)

Add caching, background jobs, separate concerns

Redis for caching and session storage. BullMQ or similar for background jobs (emails, webhooks, reports). Managed Postgres with connection pooling (PgBouncer). Daily automated backups. Basic monitoring with Grafana or Datadog. Separate the worker process from the web process.

Stage 2 — Scale ($100K–1M MRR)

Load balancing, read replicas, CDN

Multiple app server instances behind a load balancer. Postgres read replica for storefront/reporting queries. CloudFront or Cloudflare CDN for assets and storefront HTML. Containerize everything with Docker. CI/CD pipeline that deploys without downtime. On-call rotation.

Stage 3 — Enterprise ($1M+ MRR)

Kubernetes, database sharding, multi-region

K8s for container orchestration (EKS, GKE, or DOKS). Database sharding by tenant group. Redis Cluster. Multi-region active-passive or active-active. SOC2 compliance. Dedicated SRE team. SLAs with contractual uptime commitments. This is the phase where you need engineers who've done it before.

An Architecture Decision Record (ADR) is a document that captures an important architectural decision. Every non-trivial technical choice should have one. Future-you will thank present-you.

docs/decisions/ADR-0001-database-choice.md
# ADR-0001: Use PostgreSQL as Primary Database

Status: Accepted
Date: 2024-01-15
Deciders: Engineering Lead, CTO

## Context
We need a primary database for a multi-tenant SaaS.
Candidates: PostgreSQL, MySQL, MongoDB, PlanetScale (MySQL).

## Decision
Use PostgreSQL (Neon managed) for primary storage.

## Rationale
- Row Level Security (RLS) provides database-level tenant isolation
- JSONB for flexible schema columns without sacrificing queryability  
- Strong ACID guarantees for financial/order data
- Mature ecosystem, excellent tooling (Prisma, Drizzle, pgAdmin)
- Neon provides branching for dev/staging environments

## Consequences
+ Strong consistency for all tenant data
+ RLS as safety net for multi-tenancy
- MySQL-specific tools (PlanetScale features) not available
- Team must learn Postgres-specific features (RLS, JSONB, CTEs)

## Alternatives Rejected
MySQL: Weaker JSON support, no RLS, no DISTINCT ON
MongoDB: Schema flexibility not needed; ACID guarantees more important
PlanetScale: Vitess sharding valuable at scale, revisit at 10M+ records
§ 03 — Infrastructure

Hardware → Cloud → VPS — The Full Stack

Most engineers never understand what actually runs their code. From the physical server in a data center to the HTTP response in a browser — every layer explained, every choice justified.

What actually runs in a data center when your SaaS is "in the cloud."

What "4 vCPU / 8GB RAM VPS" actually means: You're getting a time-sliced portion of a large physical server (usually 96–128 cores, 256–512GB RAM) managed by a hypervisor (KVM on Linux). Your VM shares the physical CPU, but has dedicated RAM allocation. Network is shared with other tenants on the same physical switch. Storage is usually networked (NVMe over Fabrics or iSCSI) — meaning your "local" disk is actually a remote storage array.

§ 04 — Network

Network, DNS & TLS — Complete Deep Dive

How a domain name becomes an IP address, how your traffic is encrypted, and the exact sequence of events that happens before a single byte of your application code runs.

DNS (Domain Name System) is a globally distributed database that maps human-readable names to IP addresses. Understanding it deeply is essential for SaaS domain management.

TTL (Time-To-Live) is the most important DNS tuning lever. Low TTL (60s) = changes propagate fast but DNS servers make more queries. High TTL (86400s = 24h) = stale data persists but efficient. Before making a DNS change: lower TTL to 60s, wait for old TTL to expire, make the change, then raise TTL back.

How every tenant gets their own subdomain instantly — without any DNS changes per new tenant.

1

Set one wildcard A record in your DNS provider

In Cloudflare, Route53, or any DNS provider: *.yourplatform.com → A → YOUR_IP. This single record means any subdomain — whether nike.yourplatform.com or my-new-store.yourplatform.com — resolves to your server. You set this once at launch and never touch it again.

2

Get a wildcard TLS certificate

Use Let's Encrypt with DNS-01 challenge: certbot certonly --dns-cloudflare -d "*.yourplatform.com". This single certificate is valid for all subdomains. Auto-renews every 90 days. Cost: free. The DNS-01 challenge requires you to add a TXT record via your DNS provider's API — Certbot has plugins for Cloudflare, Route53, etc.

3

Your server reads the Host header

Every incoming HTTP request has a Host header. When a customer visits their-store.yourplatform.com, your Nginx or app server receives Host: their-store.yourplatform.com. Extract their-store. Query: SELECT * FROM stores WHERE subdomain = 'their-store'. Sub-millisecond lookup on indexed column.

4

New tenant = instant store URL

When a merchant signs up and picks their slug (e.g., fashionbd), you insert a row: INSERT INTO stores (subdomain, ...) VALUES ('fashionbd', ...). Their store is immediately available at fashionbd.yourplatform.com with full HTTPS — zero infrastructure work needed per tenant.

nginx.conf — Wildcard subdomain handling
# One server block handles ALL *.yourplatform.com subdomains
server {
    listen 443 ssl;
    server_name ~^(?P<subdomain>.+)\.yourplatform\.com$;

    ssl_certificate     /etc/letsencrypt/live/yourplatform.com/fullchain.pem;
    ssl_certificate_key /etc/letsencrypt/live/yourplatform.com/privkey.pem;

    location / {
        proxy_pass http://app:3000;
        # Critical: pass the original Host header to your app
        proxy_set_header Host              $host;
        proxy_set_header X-Real-IP         $remote_addr;
        proxy_set_header X-Forwarded-For   $proxy_add_x_forwarded_for;
        proxy_set_header X-Forwarded-Proto $scheme;
    }
}

Full custom domain flow — from merchant entering their domain to HTTPS traffic serving their store.

1

Merchant enters domain in admin

They type shop.theircompany.com. Your platform generates a verification token: INSERT INTO domains (store_id, hostname, verify_token) VALUES (..., 'shop.theircompany.com', 'plat-abc123'). Display instructions to merchant.

2

Merchant adds DNS records

Two records at their DNS provider: CNAME shop.theircompany.com → yourplatform.com and TXT _verify.shop.theircompany.com → "plat-abc123". The CNAME routes traffic. The TXT proves they own the domain.

3

Your platform verifies ownership

Background job: dns.resolveTxt('_verify.shop.theircompany.com') → check if it contains the token. If yes: UPDATE domains SET verified_at = NOW(). This prevents domain squatting — someone can't point your platform at a domain they don't own.

4

TLS certificate auto-provisioned

Caddy's on_demand_tls: when the first HTTPS request arrives for shop.theircompany.com, Caddy calls your /tls-check?domain=shop.theircompany.com endpoint. If you return 200, Caddy requests a Let's Encrypt cert automatically. Cert is cached. All subsequent requests use the cached cert.

5

Host header routing to correct store

Your app receives Host: shop.theircompany.com. Middleware: SELECT * FROM stores JOIN domains ON stores.id = domains.store_id WHERE domains.hostname = $host AND domains.verified_at IS NOT NULL. Returns the store, sets context. All queries scoped to that store.

middleware/domain-resolver.ts
export const resolveTenant = async (req, res, next) => {
  const host = req.headers.host?.toLowerCase();
  if (!host) return res.status(400).json({ error: 'Missing host' });

  // Check cache first (Redis key: "tenant:shop.theircompany.com")
  const cached = await redis.get(`tenant:${host}`);
  if (cached) {
    req.storeId = cached;
    return next();
  }

  // Determine if subdomain or custom domain
  const isPlatformDomain = host.endsWith('.yourplatform.com');
  let store;

  if (isPlatformDomain) {
    const subdomain = host.replace('.yourplatform.com', '');
    store = await db.stores.findUnique({ where: { subdomain } });
  } else {
    // Custom domain — must be verified
    store = await db.query(`
      SELECT s.* FROM stores s
      JOIN domains d ON s.id = d.store_id
      WHERE d.hostname = $1
        AND d.verified_at IS NOT NULL
        AND s.suspended_at IS NULL
    `, [host]);
  }

  if (!store) return res.status(404).send('Store not found');
  if (store.suspended_at) return res.status(451).send('Store suspended');

  // Cache for 60 minutes — domain lookups are expensive
  await redis.setex(`tenant:${host}`, 3600, store.id);
  req.storeId = store.id;
  req.store   = store;
  next();
};

§ 05 — Request Flow

The Complete Request Lifecycle

Every millisecond of what happens when a customer visits a store on your platform. From TCP SYN packet to rendered HTML — every layer, every decision point, every place things can go wrong.

1

DNS Resolution (0–120ms, cached: 0ms)

Browser checks OS DNS cache → router → ISP resolver → recursive lookup. Returns IP. Subsequent requests from same user: 0ms (OS cache, TTL-based). First visit to domain: 20–120ms.

2

TCP Connection (1× RTT)

3-way handshake: SYN → SYN-ACK → ACK. From Dhaka to Frankfurt (Hetzner): ~180ms RTT. From Dhaka to Singapore: ~40ms RTT. This is why server location matters for your primary user base. TCP Fast Open can eliminate this on repeat connections.

3

TLS Handshake (1–2× RTT)

TLS 1.3: 1 RTT for new sessions, 0 RTT for resumed sessions. TLS 1.2 (legacy): 2 RTTs. Certificate validation includes OCSP check (can be cached). Use TLS session tickets to skip re-negotiation for returning users.

4

CDN/Proxy Layer (0–5ms)

Cloudflare edge receives request. Checks cache: HIT returns immediately from edge (no server involved). MISS forwards to your origin server. Headers added: CF-Connecting-IP, CF-Ray. WAF rules evaluated. Rate limiting checked.

5

Load Balancer (0–1ms)

Nginx or AWS ALB selects an app server instance (round-robin, least connections, or IP hash). Health checks every 5s — unhealthy instances removed from rotation within 10–15s. Sticky sessions optional (not recommended — stateless preferred).

6

Tenant Resolution Middleware (0.5–2ms)

Your app's first job: extract Host header → look up store from Redis cache (0.2ms) or Postgres (1–2ms if cache miss) → inject store_id into request context. All subsequent middleware and route handlers read from this context.

7

Auth Middleware (0.3–1ms)

Read JWT from Authorization header or session cookie → verify signature (pure CPU, no DB) → decode payload → check if token.store_id === req.store_id (cross-store auth prevention) → attach user to request. Public routes skip this.

8

Route Handler + Business Logic (1–10ms)

Your actual code runs. Reads are served from Redis if cached. DB queries use the scoped connection with store_id filter (or RLS policy active). Complex queries may take 5–20ms. N+1 query bug (fetching related records in a loop) is the most common performance killer here.

9

Response Serialization & Compression (0.5–2ms)

JSON.stringify for APIs. HTML rendering for storefronts. Gzip/Brotli compression (Nginx handles this, reduces payload 60–80%). Set proper Cache-Control headers so CDN and browser cache correctly. Return ETag for conditional requests.

§ 06 — Multi-Tenancy

Multi-Tenancy Architecture

Multi-tenancy is the most consequential architectural decision in SaaS. Get it wrong and you either leak customer data (catastrophic) or spend months retrofitting isolation. This is the complete reference.

SHOPIFY / STRIPE / SLACK

Model A: Shared DB, Shared Schema

One Postgres instance. Every tenant's rows in the same tables. store_id UUID on every row. RLS enforces isolation at DB level.

REGULATED INDUSTRIES

Model B: Shared DB, Separate Schemas

One Postgres cluster. Each tenant has own schema (tenant_abc.products). Better isolation, harder migrations.

ENTERPRISE TIER ONLY

Model C: Database per Tenant

Dedicated Postgres per tenant. Maximum isolation. 10× operational cost. Only when enterprise customer requires it and pays for it.

PostgreSQL Row Level Security — your automatic safety net. Even if your application code has a bug and forgets the WHERE store_id clause, Postgres itself will block cross-tenant access.

migrations/001_enable_rls.sql
-- Step 1: Enable RLS on every tenant-scoped table
ALTER TABLE products  ENABLE ROW LEVEL SECURITY;
ALTER TABLE orders    ENABLE ROW LEVEL SECURITY;
ALTER TABLE customers ENABLE ROW LEVEL SECURITY;
ALTER TABLE themes    ENABLE ROW LEVEL SECURITY;
ALTER TABLE domains   ENABLE ROW LEVEL SECURITY;
-- ... repeat for ALL tenant tables

-- Step 2: Create isolation policy using session variable
-- Your app sets this at the start of each request
CREATE POLICY tenant_isolation ON products
  AS PERMISSIVE
  FOR ALL
  TO app_user
  USING (
    store_id = current_setting('app.store_id', true)::uuid
  );

-- Step 3: Superadmin role bypasses RLS (platform ops only)
CREATE POLICY superadmin_bypass ON products
  AS PERMISSIVE FOR ALL
  TO superadmin_user
  USING (true);

-- Step 4: Your app sets context before EVERY query batch
-- In your DB pool's beforeQuery hook:
SELECT set_config('app.store_id', $1, true);
-- The 'true' arg = local to transaction (resets after commit)
Critical nuance: RLS policies are per-role. If your app connects to Postgres as a superuser (postgres), RLS is bypassed for that role. Always create a dedicated app_user role with restricted permissions and connect as that role. Never connect as postgres from your application.

How tenant context flows from HTTP request through every layer of your application. This is the plumbing that makes everything else safe.

lib/tenant-context.ts — Async local storage pattern
import { AsyncLocalStorage } from 'async_hooks';

// AsyncLocalStorage: like thread-local storage but for async contexts
// Every async operation in the same request chain shares this context
const tenantStorage = new AsyncLocalStorage<{ storeId: string; plan: string }>();

// Middleware sets the context once at request entry point
export const withTenantContext = (storeId: string, plan: string, fn: () => Promise<any>) => {
  return tenantStorage.run({ storeId, plan }, fn);
};

// Any code anywhere in the call stack can access it
export const getTenantId = (): string => {
  const ctx = tenantStorage.getStore();
  if (!ctx) throw new Error('No tenant context — called outside request?');
  return ctx.storeId;
};

// DB wrapper: automatically sets RLS context before every query
export const query = async (sql: string, params: any[]) => {
  const storeId = getTenantId();
  return db.transaction(async (trx) => {
    await trx.query(`SELECT set_config('app.store_id', $1, true)`, [storeId]);
    return trx.query(sql, params);
  });
};

// Usage in route handlers — no manual store_id needed
app.get('/products', async (req, res) => {
  // getTenantId() works here, in service layer, in repository layer...
  const products = await query('SELECT * FROM products WHERE status = $1', ['active']);
  // RLS ensures only this store's products are returned
  res.json(products.rows);
});

☠ Missing store_id on a table

You add a new table and forget the store_id column. Every query on that table is cross-tenant. You won't notice until a security audit or incident. Fix: linting rule that fails migrations without store_id.

☠ Trusting client-supplied store_id

Your API accepts ?store_id=abc as a query param and uses it directly. An attacker passes a different store's ID. Always derive store_id from the authenticated session or the host header — never from user input.

☠ Shared cache keys without store_id

cache.set('products', data) — now every store gets the same product list. Cache keys must ALWAYS include store_id: products:{storeId}. This is a data leak waiting to happen.

☠ Background jobs without tenant context

A job is enqueued to "send order confirmation email" but the job payload doesn't include store_id. The worker picks it up, has no tenant context, either crashes or sends from the wrong store's config.

⚠ RLS without app-level enforcement

Relying solely on RLS without app-level store_id checks. RLS is a safety net, not the primary mechanism. If you forget to set the session variable, RLS returns zero rows (or errors) — not a security breach but a confusing bug.

⚠ Sequential IDs as resource identifiers

Using auto-increment IDs in URLs: /products/1234. Tenant A can enumerate Tenant B's products by guessing IDs. Always use UUIDs or nanoid for public-facing identifiers.

Subdomain & Custom Domain System

Complete implementation of wildcard subdomain routing, custom domain verification, SSL provisioning at scale, and domain management — everything needed to give each merchant their own branded URL.

Why domain verification matters and exactly how to implement it. Without this, anyone can point your platform at a domain they don't own — enabling phishing and brand impersonation attacks.

1

Generate a unique verification token

When a merchant submits a custom domain, generate: token = crypto.randomBytes(20).toString('hex'). Store: INSERT INTO domains (store_id, hostname, verify_token, ssl_status) VALUES ($1, $2, $3, 'pending'). Show merchant the instructions.

2

Merchant adds both DNS records

Display clearly: Record 1 (routing): CNAME shop.merchant.com → yourplatform.com. Record 2 (ownership proof): TXT _platform-verify.shop.merchant.com → "plat-{token}". Both are required. Many SaaS platforms skip the TXT check — this is a security mistake.

3

Background job polls for DNS propagation

Schedule a job: every 5 minutes, check if dns.resolveTxt('_platform-verify.shop.merchant.com') contains the token. DNS propagation takes 5 minutes to 48 hours depending on TTL. Poll with exponential backoff. Send email when verified.

4

Mark verified, trigger SSL provisioning

UPDATE domains SET verified_at = NOW(), ssl_status = 'provisioning'. Your /tls-check endpoint now returns 200 for this domain. Next HTTPS request to the domain triggers Caddy to provision the Let's Encrypt cert automatically.

5

Monitor and handle edge cases

What if merchant removes the CNAME later? Periodic recheck (daily) ensures domain still resolves to your platform. If it doesn't, mark as ssl_status = 'error' and notify merchant. Handle cert renewal (Let's Encrypt 90-day expiry) automatically via Caddy.

Migrations in a live multi-tenant system require extreme care. One wrong migration can lock tables for minutes, causing downtime for all tenants.

Never do these in production: ALTER TABLE ... ADD COLUMN NOT NULL (table lock), ALTER TABLE ... ALTER COLUMN TYPE (full table rewrite), CREATE INDEX without CONCURRENTLY (table lock during index build).
The Expand/Contract Pattern — Zero-downtime migrations
-- WRONG: Rename a column (breaks running app servers)
-- ALTER TABLE products RENAME COLUMN name TO title; ← NEVER in production

-- RIGHT: Expand → migrate → contract over multiple releases

-- Release 1: EXPAND — add new column (nullable = no lock)
ALTER TABLE products ADD COLUMN title text;

-- Release 2: App writes to BOTH old and new column
-- Background job backfills existing rows:
UPDATE products SET title = name
  WHERE title IS NULL
  LIMIT 1000;  -- batch to avoid lock

-- Release 3: App reads from new column, stops writing old one
-- Verify no nulls remain:
SELECT COUNT(*) FROM products WHERE title IS NULL;

-- Release 4: CONTRACT — add constraint, drop old column
ALTER TABLE products ALTER COLUMN title SET NOT NULL;
ALTER TABLE products DROP COLUMN name;

-- Add indexes concurrently (no table lock):
CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY idx_products_store_title
  ON products (store_id, title);

🔗 Connection Pooling (Critical)

Postgres default max_connections=100. Each app server process holds a pool. 5 app servers × 20 pool size = 100 connections = DB full. Solution: PgBouncer in transaction mode (1 connection per active query, not per client). Neon and Supabase have this built in.

📊 EXPLAIN ANALYZE

Run EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS, FORMAT JSON) on your slowest queries. Look for: "Seq Scan" on large tables (missing index), "Nested Loop" with high row estimates (N+1), high "Shared Hit Blocks" (cache misses). Fix indexes first, query structure second.

⚡ Query Optimization Rules

1) Always SELECT only needed columns (no SELECT *). 2) Use LIMIT for paginated queries. 3) Avoid functions on indexed columns in WHERE (WHERE lower(email) defeats index). 4) Use EXISTS instead of COUNT for existence checks. 5) Batch inserts with COPY or multi-row VALUES.

📈 pg_stat_statements

Enable this Postgres extension to capture query statistics: total calls, average duration, rows returned per call. Run weekly: SELECT query, calls, mean_exec_time FROM pg_stat_statements ORDER BY mean_exec_time DESC LIMIT 20; — this shows your top slow queries.

0–500 tenants

Single Postgres instance (managed)

Neon, Supabase, or Railway Postgres. No ops overhead. Connection pooling built-in. Automatic backups. Scale vertically (bigger instance) as needed. Cost: $0–$50/mo. This handles 500 tenants with 10,000 products each (5M rows) with zero performance issues if indexes are correct.

500–5K tenants

Read replica for storefront queries

Add one Postgres read replica. Route all storefront API reads (product listings, page renders) to the replica. All writes and admin queries go to primary. Cost: 2× instance price. This doubles your read capacity and reduces primary load significantly. PgBouncer or Supabase pooler on both.

5K–50K tenants

Multiple read replicas + query routing

2–5 read replicas. Intelligent query router (PgCat, or your app layer) distributes reads across replicas. Logical replication lag monitoring (alert if replica is >5s behind). Primary dedicated to writes only. Partitioning on large tables (orders by created_at month).

50K+ tenants

Horizontal sharding (Citus or manual)

Shard by store_id range or hash. Each shard is an independent Postgres instance handling a slice of tenants. Citus (now Azure Cosmos DB for Postgres) automates this. Alternatively: manual sharding with a routing table mapping store_id → shard. This is what Shopify, GitHub, and Stripe all built custom. Very complex — defer as long as possible.

export async function withCache<T>(key: string, ttl: number, fn: () => Promise<T>): Promise<T> {
  const cached = await redis.get(key);
  if (cached) return JSON.parse(cached);

  const result = await fn();
  await redis.setex(key, ttl, JSON.stringify(result));
  return result;
}

const ALLOWED_TYPES = ['image/jpeg', 'image/png', 'image/webp', 'image/gif'];
const MAX_SIZE = 10 * 1024 * 1024; // 10MB

export async function uploadProductImage(file: Buffer, storeId: string, filename: string) {
  // 1. Validate size
  if (file.length > MAX_SIZE) throw new Error('File too large');

  // 2. Detect MIME from magic bytes (not Content-Type header — that's spoofable)
  const detected = await fileTypeFromBuffer(file);
  if (!detected || !ALLOWED_TYPES.includes(detected.mime)) {
    throw new Error(`Unsupported file type: ${detected?.mime}`);
  }

  // 3. Namespaced key: store_id prevents any cross-tenant file access
  const key = `stores/${storeId}/products/${nanoid()}.${detected.ext}`;

  await r2.send(new PutObjectCommand({
    Bucket: 'platform-media',
    Key: key,
    Body: file,
    ContentType: detected.mime,
    // Cache for 1 year (immutable — new key per upload)
    CacheControl: 'public, max-age=31536000, immutable',
  }));

  return `https://media.yourplatform.com/${key}`;
}

H

Cleaner URLs. Version is explicit in HTTP header. Harder to test in browser. Used by GitHub API v3. More complex to route in middleware.

S

§ 12 — Security

Authentication & Authorization

Four completely separate auth contexts, JWT implementation, OAuth 2.0, RBAC, and the critical rule that separates secure SaaS from the rest: a token from Store A must never grant access to Store B's data.

Four completely separate authentication systems. They must never share JWT secrets, session stores, or user tables.

A. Merchant Auth (Admin Dashboard)

Staff login at admin.yourplatform.com. JWT payload: {userId, storeId, role, exp}. Both userId AND storeId must match. Role determines permissions within that store only. Refresh token stored in DB (revocable).

B. Customer Auth (Storefront)

Customer accounts are per-store. customers table has store_id. A customer at Store A has a completely separate identity from a customer at Store B (even with the same email). JWT payload: {customerId, storeId, exp}.

C. API Key Auth (Headless/Integrations)

Two key types per store: pk_live_ (public, read-only storefront data) and sk_live_ (secret, full admin access). Keys are hashed in DB (bcrypt). Shown once on creation. Rotatable. Rate limited per key.

D. Platform Admin Auth (Super-admin)

IP-restricted endpoint. Separate JWT signing secret. Mandatory TOTP MFA. Hardware key support (YubiKey). Every action audit-logged with admin identity, IP, timestamp. Completely isolated from merchant auth — different domain, different secret, different user table.

lib/auth/jwt.ts — Secure JWT implementation
import jwt from 'jsonwebtoken';

// Use RS256 (asymmetric) for tokens verified by multiple services
// Use HS256 (symmetric) for single-service tokens — simpler
const JWT_SECRET = process.env.JWT_SECRET; // 256-bit random, from Secrets Manager

export function issueToken(payload: {
  userId: string;
  storeId: string;  // CRITICAL: always include
  role: string;
  type: 'access' | 'refresh';
}) {
  return jwt.sign(payload, JWT_SECRET, {
    expiresIn: payload.type === 'access' ? '15m' : '30d',
    issuer: 'yourplatform.com',
    audience: 'yourplatform-api',
  });
}

export function verifyToken(token: string, expectedStoreId: string) {
  const payload = jwt.verify(token, JWT_SECRET, {
    issuer: 'yourplatform.com',
    audience: 'yourplatform-api',
  }) as any;

  // CRITICAL: Verify token belongs to the store being accessed
  // Without this, a token from Store A grants access to Store B
  if (payload.storeId !== expectedStoreId) {
    throw new Error('Token store mismatch — potential cross-tenant attack');
  }

  return payload;
}

§ 13 — Async Systems

Background Jobs & Event-Driven Architecture

Any work that doesn't need to complete before returning an HTTP response belongs in a queue. This section covers job architecture, reliability patterns, and when to graduate from simple queues to event streaming.

🔄 Fan-Out Pattern

One event triggers multiple jobs. Order placed → email confirmation + inventory update + analytics event + webhook delivery. Enqueue each as a separate job. Failure of one doesn't block others. BullMQ supports this with job dependencies.

⏱️ Scheduled Jobs

Cron-like jobs: daily invoice generation, weekly analytics digest, expired token cleanup, subscription renewal reminders. BullMQ has repeat options. Always make scheduled jobs idempotent — if they run twice, the result is the same.

🔁 Idempotency

Jobs must be safely retryable. Use idempotency keys: before processing, check if a job with this key was already processed. Store result in Redis (TTL: 24h). If already done, return cached result. Critical for payment webhooks — duplicate events happen.

💀 Dead Letter Queue

Jobs that exhaust all retries go to a dead letter queue (DLQ). Never silently discard failed jobs. DLQ should trigger an alert and be manually reviewable. Critical for payment-related jobs — you need to know if an order confirmation was never sent.

Rule 1

At-least-once delivery

BullMQ guarantees at-least-once delivery. Your workers must handle duplicate job execution safely. Every job handler should be idempotent.

Rule 2

Exponential backoff

Don't retry immediately. Wait 2s, then 4s, then 8s, then 16s, then 32s. This prevents thundering herd when a downstream service (email provider, payment gateway) is degraded.

Rule 3

Job timeout

Every job must have a maximum execution time. If a job runs longer than 30 seconds, something is wrong. Set lockDuration: 30000 in BullMQ. Stalled jobs are automatically retried.

Rule 4

Observability

Monitor queue depth, job processing rate, failure rate, and processing latency per queue. A growing queue depth without growing throughput means your workers are overwhelmed — scale them horizontally.

BullMQ vs Kafka — when to upgrade: BullMQ (Redis-backed) handles 10,000 jobs/second easily and covers 95% of SaaS needs. Move to Kafka/SQS when: you need event replay (replay all events from 3 days ago), fan-out to 50+ consumers, multi-region event distribution, or compliance audit trail of all events. Most SaaS platforms never need Kafka. GitHub moved from BullMQ-equivalent to Kafka at 50M+ events/day.

§ 19 — Security

Security Architecture

Security in a multi-tenant SaaS is not a feature you add at the end. Every decision — from how you store credentials to how you write database queries — has security implications. This is the complete defense model.

☠ Tenant Data Leak

Severity: Critical. Missing WHERE store_id clause returns all tenants' data. Prevention: Postgres RLS as safety net, AsyncLocalStorage for tenant context, linting rules that flag queries without store_id filter, integration tests that verify isolation.

☠ Credential Theft

Severity: Critical. DB contains every merchant's payment API keys. If stolen, attacker can drain every merchant's payment account. Prevention: AES-256-GCM encryption, key stored in Secrets Manager (not DB), decrypt only in memory of payment service.

⚠ Subdomain Takeover

Deleted store's subdomain is reclaimed by attacker. Prevention: subdomain lockout period (30 days after deletion), soft-delete pattern, reserved subdomain list.

⚠ Merchant XSS via Theme

If merchants can inject raw HTML, they can steal customer payment data from other stores. Prevention: theme stored as structured JSON, never raw HTML. All merchant text inputs sanitized with DOMPurify before storage.

→ Noisy Neighbor DoS

One store floods your API with traffic, starving all other stores. Prevention: per-store rate limiting at API gateway level, circuit breakers per store, resource quotas per plan tier.

→ Supply Chain Attack

Malicious npm package compromise. Prevention: Dependabot/Renovate for automated updates, npm audit in CI, lockfile committed, specific version pins for critical packages, SBOM generation.

Strategy 1 — Blue/Green

Two identical environments, switch traffic

Blue = live, Green = new version. Deploy to Green. Run smoke tests. Switch load balancer to Green. Blue stays warm for instant rollback. Zero downtime. Used by Netflix, Amazon, GitHub. Requires 2× infra cost during deployment window (minutes).

Strategy 2 — Rolling

Replace instances one at a time

Kill one old instance, bring up one new instance, repeat. Lower resource cost than blue/green. Brief period where both old and new versions handle traffic — code must be backward compatible during transition. Works well with Kubernetes rolling deployments.

Strategy 3 — Canary

Route small percentage to new version

1% → 5% → 25% → 100% of traffic goes to new version. Monitor error rates at each step. Automatic rollback if error rate spikes. Best for high-risk changes. Requires sophisticated load balancer routing (Nginx plus, Istio, AWS ALB weighted target groups).

§ 22 — Operations

Monitoring & Observability

The three pillars — Logs, Metrics, and Traces — plus the alerting that wakes you up before your customers notice problems. In a multi-tenant SaaS, always filter by store_id.

📋 Logs — What happened

Structured JSON logs. Every log line includes: timestamp, level, store_id, user_id, request_id, message, duration_ms. Never log PII (emails, passwords, payment info). Tool: Axiom, Papertrail, or self-hosted Loki. Query by store_id for per-tenant debugging.

📊 Metrics — How the system behaves

Request rate, error rate, response time (p50/p95/p99), queue depth, DB connection count, cache hit rate — all tagged with store_id for per-tenant view. Tool: Prometheus + Grafana or Datadog. Alert on error rate > 1% or p99 > 500ms.

🔍 Traces — Why it's slow

Distributed tracing connects a single request across API server → DB → cache → external APIs. Shows exactly where 200ms went. OpenTelemetry is the standard. Trace ID propagated in all log lines and downstream requests. Tool: Jaeger, Tempo, or Datadog APM.

🚨 Errors — What broke

Sentry or Highlight.io for exception tracking. Every unhandled error includes: stack trace, store_id, user_id, request context. Group similar errors. Alert when new error type first appears. Never alert on known/expected errors (404, validation).

§ 23 — Scale

Scaling Patterns

How to identify bottlenecks before they become incidents, and how to scale each layer independently. The bottlenecks always appear in a predictable order — learn to anticipate them.

Bottleneck #1 — Database connections

Appears at: 3–5 app server instances

Each app server holds a connection pool (10–20 connections). 5 servers × 20 = 100 connections = Postgres limit. Fix: PgBouncer in transaction mode. Neon and Supabase have this built in. Cost: zero. Impact: handle 10× more app servers with same DB.

Bottleneck #2 — Database read throughput

Appears at: ~500K page views/day

Primary DB handles all reads and writes. Read-heavy workloads (storefront page requests) overwhelm it. Fix: Postgres read replica. Route storefront queries to replica. Writes still go to primary. Async replication lag is acceptable for storefront (eventual consistency is fine for product listings).

Bottleneck #3 — App server CPU

Appears at: sustained high traffic

Single app server CPU maxes out. Fix: Horizontal scaling — more app server instances behind a load balancer. Stateless app servers scale linearly. Move session state to Redis (not in-memory). Separate web workers from CPU-intensive background workers.

Bottleneck #4 — Database write throughput

Appears at: 5M+ orders/day

Primary DB write throughput maxes out. Fix: This is the hard one. Options: Postgres partitioning (horizontal partitioning by date), Citus (parallel Postgres), or manual sharding by store_id ranges. This is where Shopify's "pod architecture" becomes necessary. Each pod handles a shard of stores. Requires years of planning and a dedicated DB team.

Scaling sequence: 1) Fix queries (indexes) before scaling infra. 2) Cache before scaling DB. 3) Scale horizontally before scaling vertically. 4) Profile before optimizing. 90% of "scaling problems" are actually missing indexes or N+1 query bugs.

import { createCipheriv, createDecipheriv, randomBytes, scrypt } from 'crypto';
import { promisify } from 'util';

const ALGO = 'aes-256-gcm';
// Key from Secrets Manager — 32 bytes (256 bits)
const KEY = Buffer.from(process.env.ENCRYPTION_KEY!, 'hex');

// Encrypt: returns iv:authTag:ciphertext (all hex, colon-separated)
export function encrypt(plaintext: string): string {
  const iv = randomBytes(12);          // 96-bit IV, unique per encryption
  const cipher = createCipheriv(ALGO, KEY, iv);
  const enc = Buffer.concat([cipher.update(plaintext, 'utf8'), cipher.final()]);
  const tag = cipher.getAuthTag();       // 16-byte authentication tag (GCM)
  return [iv, tag, enc].map(b => b.toString('hex')).join(':');
}

// Decrypt: reverses encrypt()
export function decrypt(stored: string): string {
  const [ivHex, tagHex, dataHex] = stored.split(':');
  const decipher = createDecipheriv(ALGO, KEY, Buffer.from(ivHex, 'hex'));
  decipher.setAuthTag(Buffer.from(tagHex, 'hex'));  // verifies integrity
  return decipher.update(dataHex, 'hex', 'utf8') + decipher.final('utf8');
}

import { db } from '@repo/db';

// Repository: ONLY place with DB queries. No HTTP, no business logic.
export const productRepo = {

  async findMany(storeId: string, opts: { status?: string; cursor?: string; limit?: number }) {
    return db.product.findMany({
      where: {
        storeId,   // tenant scope — ALWAYS
        status: opts.status ?? 'active',
        ...(opts.cursor ? { createdAt: { lt: new Date(opts.cursor) } } : {}),
      },
      orderBy: { createdAt: 'desc' },
      take: opts.limit ?? 20,
    });
  },

  async findBySlug(storeId: string, slug: string) {
    return db.product.findFirst({
      where: { storeId, slug, status: 'active' }
    });
  },

  async create(storeId: string, data: CreateProductInput) {
    const slug = slugify(data.title);
    return db.product.create({
      data: { ...data, storeId, slug, status: 'draft' }
    });
  },

  async update(storeId: string, id: string, data: UpdateProductInput) {
    // The where clause scopes to storeId — prevents updating other store's products
    return db.product.update({
      where: { id, storeId },  // Prisma throws if not found (cross-tenant safe)
      data,
    });
  },

  async delete(storeId: string, id: string) {
    return db.product.update({
      where: { id, storeId },
      data: { status: 'archived', deletedAt: new Date() }
      // Soft delete — never hard DELETE (order history needs product data)
    });
  },
};

export const checkoutService = {

  // Step 1: Validate cart items and reserve inventory
  async validateCart(storeId: string, items: CartItem[]) {
    const productIds = items.map(i => i.productId);
    const products = await productRepo.findByIds(storeId, productIds);

    for (const item of items) {
      const product = products.find(p => p.id === item.productId);
      if (!product) throw new ApiError(404, 'PRODUCT_NOT_FOUND', `Product not found`);
      if (product.inventoryQty < item.quantity) {
        throw new ApiError(400, 'INSUFFICIENT_STOCK', `${product.title}: only ${product.inventoryQty} in stock`);
      }
    }
    return products;
  },

  // Step 2: Create the order (status: pending)
  async createOrder(storeId: string, cart: Cart, customer: Customer) {
    const orderNumber = await orderRepo.nextOrderNumber(storeId);
    return orderRepo.create({
      storeId, orderNumber, status: 'pending',
      customerId: customer.id,
      lineItems: cart.items.map(i => ({        // SNAPSHOT — immutable
        productId: i.productId, title: i.product.title,
        price: i.product.price, quantity: i.quantity,
      })),
      total: calculateTotal(cart),
    });
  },

  // Step 3: Initiate payment
  async initiatePayment(storeId: string, orderId: string) {
    const [order, pm] = await Promise.all([
      orderRepo.findById(storeId, orderId),
      paymentRepo.getActive(storeId),
    ]);
    const creds = decrypt(pm.credentials);
    const gateway = gatewayRegistry[pm.provider];
    return gateway.createPayment(order, creds);
  },

  // Step 4: Payment confirmed (called from webhook)
  async confirmPayment(storeId: string, orderId: string, txId: string) {
    await orderRepo.update(storeId, orderId, { status: 'paid', paymentTransactionId: txId });

    // Fan-out: multiple independent jobs
    await Promise.all([
      queueJob('order_confirmation_email', { orderId, storeId }),
      queueJob('inventory_decrement', { orderId, storeId }),
      queueJob('generate_invoice', { orderId, storeId }),
      queueJob('analytics_order_completed', { orderId, storeId }),
    ]);
  },

export const domainService = {

  async addCustomDomain(storeId: string, hostname: string) {
    // Validate: not a reserved domain, valid format
    if (RESERVED.test(hostname)) throw new ApiError(400, 'RESERVED_HOSTNAME', 'Domain not allowed');
    if (!VALID_HOSTNAME.test(hostname)) throw new ApiError(400, 'INVALID_HOSTNAME', 'Invalid format');

    const token = generateToken(20); // crypto.randomBytes(20).toString('hex')
    const domain = await domainRepo.create({
      storeId, hostname, verifyToken: token, sslStatus: 'pending'
    });

    // Schedule verification job (polls DNS every 5 min)
    await queueJob('verify_domain', { domainId: domain.id, storeId }, {
      repeat: { every: 5 * 60 * 1000 }, // every 5 minutes
      attempts: 288,  // try for 24 hours
    });

    return { domain, instructions: {
      cname: { host: hostname, value: 'yourplatform.com' },
      txt:   { host: `_platform-verify.${hostname}`, value: `plat-${token}` },
    }};
  },

  async verifyDomain(domainId: string, storeId: string) {
    const domain = await domainRepo.findById(domainId, storeId);
    if (!domain || domain.verifiedAt) return; // already verified

    try {
      const records = await dns.resolveTxt(`_platform-verify.${domain.hostname}`);
      const flat = records.flat();
      if (!flat.includes(`plat-${domain.verifyToken}`)) return; // not yet

      await domainRepo.update(domainId, { verifiedAt: new Date(), sslStatus: 'provisioning' });
      await queueJob('domain_verified_email', { domainId, storeId });
      // Caddy will provision SSL cert on next HTTPS request
    } catch (e) {
      // DNS not propagated yet — job will retry in 5 minutes
    }
  },

  // /tls-check endpoint — called by Caddy on-demand TLS
  async isTLSAllowed(hostname: string): Promise<boolean> {
    const domain = await domainRepo.findByHostname(hostname);
    return !!(domain?.verifiedAt && !domain.store.suspendedAt);
  },
};

Production Operations Runbook

The playbooks your on-call engineer follows at 3am. Written when calm, executed under pressure. Every incident you handle without a runbook is harder than it needs to be.

The first 15 minutes of an incident determine whether it lasts 30 minutes or 4 hours. This is the playbook.

1

Acknowledge (0–2 min)

Acknowledge the alert. Post in #incidents Slack channel: "Investigating high error rate alert. Taking point." This prevents duplicate investigation. Check the dashboard — is this real or a monitoring blip?

2

Assess blast radius (2–5 min)

How many stores affected? Is it all tenants or specific ones? Is checkout broken (revenue impact) or just analytics (low impact)? Check: error rate by endpoint, which store_ids appear in error logs, Stripe webhook failures, DB connection pool status.

3

Communicate (5 min)

Update status page with: "We are investigating elevated error rates affecting some storefronts. Checkouts are [functional/impacted]. Updates every 15 minutes." Merchants deserve honesty. Silence breeds panic and support tickets.

4

Mitigate first, fix second (5–15 min)

Can you rollback to the last stable deployment? Do it first. Can you disable the broken feature flag? Do it. Can you route traffic away from the broken region? Do it. Restore service, then investigate root cause. Never debug in production while users are down.

5

Post-mortem (next day)

Blameless post-mortem: timeline of events, root cause, contributing factors, what worked, what didn't, action items with owners and due dates. Share with the whole team. The goal is systemic improvement, not finger-pointing.

The most important on-call rule: When in doubt, roll back. A rollback that takes 90 seconds is always better than debugging for 90 minutes with users down. You can always redeploy the fix once you understand the root cause.

FROM pg_stat_activity
WHERE datname = 'yourplatform'
GROUP BY state, wait_event_type, wait_event;

SELECT pid, now() - pg_stat_activity.query_start AS duration,
       query, state, wait_event
FROM pg_stat_activity
WHERE (now() - pg_stat_activity.query_start) > interval '30 seconds'
  AND state != 'idle';

-- Kill a specific long-running query (not terminate — softer)
SELECT pg_cancel_backend(pid) FROM pg_stat_activity WHERE pid = 12345;

-- Check table bloat (over time, dead rows accumulate)
SELECT schemaname, tablename,
       n_dead_tup, n_live_tup,
       round(n_dead_tup::numeric/nullif(n_live_tup+n_dead_tup,0)*100, 1) AS dead_pct
FROM pg_stat_user_tables
WHERE n_dead_tup > 10000
ORDER BY n_dead_tup DESC;

-- Manual VACUUM if dead rows > 20% (autovacuum may be behind)
VACUUM ANALYZE products;

SELECT schemaname, tablename, indexname, idx_scan, idx_tup_read
FROM pg_stat_user_indexes
WHERE idx_scan = 0   -- zero scans = never used
